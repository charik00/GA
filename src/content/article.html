Medicine or supplement: not what we thought. Part 1

Why modern pharmacology fails with chronic conditions

We are used to a simple division: medicine treats, a supplement supports. But the closer you look at how the body actually absorbs what we give it, the more this line blurs, and the more questions the modern approach leaves unanswered.

From nature to synthetic

[Placeholder paragraph, pour the full text here later.] For most of human history, remedies came from whole plants: complexes of dozens of compounds that evolved together and that the body learned to read as a single signal. Twentieth-century pharmacology took a different path, isolate the single “active” molecule, synthesise it at scale, and discard everything around it as inert. It was efficient. It was also a quiet bet that the rest of the plant didn’t matter.

That bet is now under question. The compounds we removed turn out to govern how the “active” one is absorbed, transported, and used.

Vitamin C is not just ascorbic acid

[Placeholder paragraph.] On a label, “vitamin C” and “ascorbic acid” look interchangeable. In a citrus fruit they are not. Natural vitamin C arrives wrapped in a matrix that shapes how the body recognises and retains it:

  • bioflavonoids that protect the molecule and extend its activity;
  • enzymes and cofactors that direct it to the tissues that need it;
  • trace minerals present in precise, naturally occurring ratios.

Strip all of that away and you are left with a single acid. The number on the bottle may be identical; what the body does with it is not.

The question was never how much of a molecule we deliver, but whether the body can recognise it at all.

B12: the number is there, the deficiency remains

[Placeholder paragraph.] This is the paradox that sends people back to the clinic. Bloodwork shows B12 within range, supplementation is “adequate” on paper, and the symptoms of deficiency persist. The measured quantity and the usable quantity have come apart, because absorption depends on a chain of cofactors that an isolated dose simply doesn’t bring with it.
